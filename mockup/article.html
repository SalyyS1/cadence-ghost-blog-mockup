The 5AM Argument: Why Easy Runs Should Feel Boring

Eight weeks of running slower than felt reasonable — and the 4:12 half marathon that came out the other side. Here's the heart-rate data, the doubts, and what I'd do differently.

For two years I ran every easy run at 5:20 per kilometre because that was the pace that felt like running. Anything slower felt like admitting something. Then a coach looked at eight weeks of my data and said the least flattering thing anyone has said about my training: none of it was easy, and none of it was hard.

That's the trap. If every run sits in the same middling zone, you get the fatigue cost of hard training with none of the adaptation. The fix is boring, and it is genuinely difficult to do: slow the easy runs down until they feel almost embarrassing, so the hard sessions can actually be hard.

The experiment

Eight weeks. Four runs a week. Three of them capped at 138 bpm — no exceptions, even on hills, even when it meant walking. One session per week genuinely hard. I kept weekly volume roughly where it had been, around 52 km, so the only variable that changed was intensity distribution.

The first fortnight was miserable in a way I hadn't expected. Not physically — physically it was easy, that was the point. It was the pace on the watch. 6:10 per kilometre on a flat road in good weather, being overtaken by people who were, I told myself, clearly doing intervals.

By week three the same heart rate was producing a pace 25 seconds per kilometre faster. Nothing else in my life had changed.
Week 3 · Zone 2 progression

What the data showed

Aerobic decoupling is the number I ended up caring about: how much your heart rate drifts upward over a long run while pace stays constant. Under 5% means the effort is genuinely aerobic. Mine started at 9.4% and finished the block at 3.1%.

Coach's note

If your easy pace and your half-marathon pace are within 45 seconds per kilometre of each other, you don't have an easy pace — you have one gear. Slow the easy days by 30 seconds and hold it for three weeks before judging the result.

Race day

I went through 10 km in 53:40, which was 40 seconds down on goal and felt, for the first time in any race I've run, comfortable. The second half took 58:28 including a headwind on the exposed stretch after the bridge. Final time 1:52:08 — a personal best by four minutes on a course that wasn't faster.

What I'd do differently

  • Cap by feel first, heart rate second. Chest-strap data on cold mornings gave me two phantom spikes that made me walk when I didn't need to.
  • Add the strides in week one, not week four. Eight weeks of pure slow running left my legs flat the first time I tried threshold work.
  • Stop checking splits mid-run. The pace is a result, not a target. I lost two easy runs to arguing with my watch.

None of this is new. Every coaching book has said it for forty years. The only thing worth adding is that the hard part isn't physiological, it's social — running slowly in public, on purpose, for two months. That's the whole cost. It bought me four minutes.
